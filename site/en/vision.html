Product vision

Decompose and orchestrate: unlocking the true value of AI.

Why AI started where it did

The architecture its limits allowed.

The first generation of AI in customer service was built around a hard constraint: limited context and less capable models. Two approaches made sense, and both delivered — using AI to replace individual tools (a bot instead of an IVR, a classifier, a summarizer) and building narrowly scoped specialist agents. The more focused the task, the better the results, because it stayed within the model's reach. That wasn't a misstep; it was the right architecture for its limits. Those limits have now lifted.

Why a bigger model isn't enough

A larger context is necessary — not sufficient.

The obvious temptation is to load an entire process into one enormous context and let the model handle it. But three things haven't changed, and each one undermines that idea:

Large contexts degrade

Detail gets lost inside an oversized context. A smaller, focused one is consistently more accurate and reliable.

Long cycles are about state, not size

A process that spans days, moves across sessions and waits on other people won't fit in any context. It needs to persist and resume — not more tokens.

Cost

A full context is reprocessed on every call, so you pay for all of it even when a step uses a fraction.

The shift

Decompose agents + workflow.

Apply to your processes what engineering has always done with complexity — break them down — and coordinate the pieces: an orchestrator that calls on whichever specialist owns each step; specialists you can reuse (human and AI, in-house and third-party); and a workflow that holds state and connects the steps over time. This moves both levers of ROI at once:

Cost drops by design

Each step carries only the context it needs and runs on the right model — inexpensive for routine work, costly only where genuine reasoning is required. Steps that need no AI at all, like a system call or a rule, incur no inference cost.

Impact grows — decomposing is a chance to redesign

Most of the value is lost when you automate an existing human process unchanged. Break it apart and every step becomes clear enough to ask the real question: does this still need to exist? The gain is in the process you make shorter.

Redesigning doesn't conflict with reuse. The redesign happens in the composition — the workflow that reorders or removes steps — while the specialists remain reusable building blocks, recombined in a new sequence. That's precisely why decomposition makes redesign inexpensive. And the few blocks that don't make the cut were only there to work around a human limitation in the first place.

The outcome

Lower cost, greater impact, real return.

Returns improve from both directions: decomposition reduces cost (fewer tokens, the right model per step, inference only where it adds value) and increases impact — the value of the entire redesigned process, not of a single answer. And because every step is specific, it's measurable and auditable: the confidence you need to finally trust automation with a real process.

This is where people fit best — as one more specialist who begins in control and steps back only as fast as the evidence allows:

STEP 1

People in control

The process begins led by people, with AI assisting wherever it's already proven safe.

STEP 2

AI takes on what's proven

Each step that evaluation confirms as reliable moves over to automation.

STEP 3

A measured, reversible handover

People step back only as fast as the evidence allows — and step right back in if confidence drops.
